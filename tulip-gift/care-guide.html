🌷 Your Tulip Care Guide

Growing Beautiful
Tulips Every Year

Dutch tulips are perennials — with a little care each season, your bulbs will come back bigger and brighter year after year. Here's everything you need to know, from the moment you plant your bulbs all the way through to the next bloom.

🍂 Start in fall
❄️ Cold = blooms
🌞 Full sun preferred
✂️ Deadhead after bloom
📦 Lift & store in summer
🔁 Repeat every year
Step 1 — Fall
Plant Your Bulbs in Autumn
1

Wait for the Right Temperature

Plant in October or November, once overnight temperatures have consistently dipped below 50°F. Planting too early in warm soil can trigger premature sprouting, which leaves new growth vulnerable to hard freezes.

2

Choose the Right Spot

Pick a location that gets at least 6 hours of direct sunlight and has well-draining soil. Tulips hate sitting in soggy ground — if your soil holds water, mix in some coarse sand or plant in a raised bed.

3

Plant Correctly

Dig holes 6–8 inches deep and place each bulb pointed-side up. Space bulbs 4–6 inches apart. Backfill with soil, press gently to eliminate air pockets, then give the bed a thorough watering. That's the last watering they'll need until spring.

🌍 About Your Dutch Bulbs

Your bulbs were sourced from the Netherlands, where tulip growing has been perfected over 400 years. Dutch bulbs are carefully conditioned during export, but planting them in cold fall soil is still essential — that cold period (called vernalization) is what triggers the bloom. Trust the process.

Step 2 — Winter
Underground Magic
1

Leave Them Alone

In most climates (zones 5–7) no action is needed at all. Your bulbs are vernalizing — undergoing the cold exposure that cues them to bloom. Resist any urge to check on them or water them.

2

Mulch in Very Cold Climates

If you're in zone 4 or colder, apply a 2–3 inch layer of straw or shredded leaves over the bed after the ground freezes. This regulates soil temperature and prevents the freeze-thaw cycles that can heave bulbs out of the ground.

3

Watch for Green Tips in Late Winter

By late February or March you may spot small green points pushing through the soil. This is a great sign — your bulbs have done their cold work and are waking up. Remove any mulch at this point so the shoots can grow freely.

Step 3 — Spring
Bloom Season & Deadheading
1

Water at the Base, Not Overhead

As shoots grow and buds form, water only at soil level if conditions are dry. Wetting the foliage and petals encourages fungal issues. Once blooms open, you can mostly let spring rains do the work.

2

Deadhead at the Right Moment

Once petals begin to drop or look papery — usually 2–3 weeks after opening — it's time to deadhead. Leaving the spent bloom on the plant signals it to put energy into making seeds instead of recharging the bulb.

3

Snip Just the Flower Head

Using clean scissors or pruners, cut the stem just below the flower head, right above the first leaf. Remove only the bloom — not the stem or leaves. The green parts are still feeding the bulb through photosynthesis and must stay on the plant.

4

Leave the Foliage Alone

This is the most important rule of the whole growing season: do not cut back the leaves until they have turned fully yellow and fallen over on their own — usually about 6 weeks after the bloom. Cutting them early is the single most common reason tulips fail to return the following year.

💡 Hiding the Dying Foliage

If the yellowing leaves feel untidy, try planting low-growing perennials like creeping phlox or hostas nearby. They'll fill in around the tulips and camouflage the foliage as it fades, without competing with or disturbing the bulbs.

Step 4 — Early Summer
Lifting & Storing Your Bulbs
1

Wait for Leaves to Fully Yellow

Resist the urge to dig early. Wait until the leaves have gone fully yellow or brown and laid flat — typically late May to mid-June. At this point the bulb has absorbed all the nutrients it can and is ready to be lifted.

2

Dig Carefully

Use a garden fork or trowel and work in a generous circle about 6 inches out from the stem to avoid nicking the bulb. Loosen the soil and gently lift. You may find small "daughter" bulbs attached — these can be replanted separately and will bloom in 1–2 years.

3

Cure for 3–5 Days

Brush off excess soil (don't wash the bulbs) and spread them in a single layer in a shaded, well-ventilated spot for 3–5 days. This curing period dries the outer skin and prevents rot during storage. Discard any bulbs that feel mushy, look diseased, or have lost their papery outer tunic.

🌡️

Store Cool & Dry

Keep bulbs somewhere with temperatures between 60–70°F and low humidity — a garage, shed, or cool basement shelf works great. Moisture is the enemy of stored bulbs.

🧺

Use a Breathable Container

Store in a mesh bag, old pantyhose, a wooden crate with slats, or a cardboard box with ventilation holes. Never use sealed plastic or airtight containers — bulbs need airflow to prevent mold.

🚫

Keep Away from Fruit

Do not store bulbs near apples, pears, or other ripening fruit. Ripening fruit releases ethylene gas, which can damage the flower developing inside the bulb and cause deformed or missing blooms next spring.

🔍

Check Monthly

Give bulbs a quick look every few weeks and remove any that develop soft spots, mold, or an off-putting smell. One bad bulb can spread rot to its neighbors.

🏷️

Label Your Varieties

With multiple varieties in the mix, pop a small label on each group with the variety name. It makes fall planting much more intentional — you'll know exactly what color is going where before the first bulb hits the soil.

🔁 Then Repeat from Step 1

Once October rolls around and overnight temps drop below 50°F, you're back to Step 1. Plant your stored bulbs and do it all over again — year after year, each spring more established than the last.

Common Questions
Troubleshooting & Tips
?

My tulips came up but didn't bloom — why?

The most common causes are cutting the foliage back too early the previous year (starving the bulb of nutrients), planting too shallow, or insufficient cold during vernalization. Bulbs need at least 12–14 weeks below 50°F to trigger a bloom.

?

Do I have to lift bulbs every year?

In warm climates (zone 8 and warmer) yes — summer heat and humidity will rot them in the ground. In cooler climates (zones 4–7) you can leave them, though lifting and storing tends to produce bigger, more reliable blooms year after year.

?

Squirrels keep digging up my bulbs!

A few tricks that work well: lay chicken wire flat over the planting area (remove in spring), sprinkle crushed red pepper flakes in the soil, or plant bulbs beneath a layer of gravel. Sprinkling blood meal fertilizer on the soil surface also deters most rodents.

?

Can I use them as cut flowers?

Absolutely! Cut tulips when the bud is still closed but fully colored, trimming the stem at an angle. Place in cool water and they'll open beautifully indoors. Keep them away from warm spots and direct sun — tulips actually prefer cool temperatures and will last 7–10 days in a vase.
